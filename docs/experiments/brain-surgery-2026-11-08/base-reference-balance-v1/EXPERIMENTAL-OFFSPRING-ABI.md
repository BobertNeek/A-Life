# Experimental offspring compatibility finding

A disposable composite-genetics test reproduced two experimental-foundation parents into one child (conception seed 703). Each parent's expressed genome was individually admitted against the shared asset, but compiling the child's expressed genome against it returned `PhenotypeCompile`, before archiving or GPU admission. A separate unbound experimental-graph compilation of the same child succeeded. Comparing its freshly generated manifest to the parent asset showed matching layout and persistent-address/route identities, but different action-decoder and plasticity ABI digests. Thus a raw reuse of the parent asset cannot be assumed safe even though addresses appear stable: genetic variation changes semantics relevant to the weights.

The founder's own composite genetic archive and portable restore passed separate CPU tests after explicit experimental compiler routing. Those tests do not establish offspring support. Do not weaken the manifest identity checks or silently freeze child mutations. A safe solution needs to establish exactly which changed action and plasticity fields are compatible with inherited weight coordinates and bind any appropriately reconstructed offspring asset to the child's own ABI, with end-to-end birth, archive, reload and learning tests. No user life was modified.

A follow-up comparison in this single parent/child fixture found all 32,768 indexed synapses had matching source, target, route and compiled kind, and matching receptor indices; 28,672 alpha values differed. The sensor encoder and candidate decoder objects also differed, while the memory decoder, persistent-address digest and route digest matched. These observations explain why a compatibility-preserving child-specific binding may be worth investigating, but they do not establish correctness across genomes or justify ignoring changed sensory, decision or plasticity semantics. In addition, current reproduction requires both parents' candidate assets to be equal, so simply issuing a different asset for each child could prevent later mating. Treat the inherited-weight and cross-parent compatibility design as one coupled problem.

A guarded rebind prototype was tested and rejected this child: although every indexed synapse matched both parents structurally, their compiled sensor plans had 183 assignments versus the child's 187 and many source-to-neuron assignments moved. This is not merely different encoder scale or bias; wholesale sensor remapping means the same recurrent weights can receive different inputs. The prototype was reverted and was never published to the PR. A safe solution must address sensory-port semantics, not just reuse persistent synapse addresses. The sample was one parent pair and does not establish the frequency of this issue.
