# Full-life fast-write control pilot

One fresh seed 2026111541, near food and far food, original and two-weight boosted founders, 64 decisions each, normal vs fast-write sham: eight native CPU trials, complete. The same research-only binary computed the waking update in both modes; sham suppressed its fast writes while retaining eligibility and the normal generation bookkeeping. No user or production state was touched. This local environment switch is not a production feature or an eligible gameplay change. Memory, predictor, recurrent activations, biochemical processes and other learning remained active; no equal wall-clock cost was measured.

For all four pairs, initial candidate identities, initial logits and first sampled action matched exactly. Normal fast entries changed in 26,624 positions; sham fast entries remained unchanged, while eligibility changed in 26,624 in both. All 64 sampled representative and channel choices matched in each pair, candidate identities matched at each step, and both modes consumed the food in each scene. Maximum absolute candidate-logit differences over the respective trajectories were 0.01125–0.01289. All eight exact cold-next-step and unchanged-founder-optimizer checks passed. The data show that removing fast writes can alter neural scores without altering the sampled behavior in these simple one-seed scenes. They do not prove that fast plasticity is ineffective elsewhere, nor that learning is useful or useless in a novel-cue task. No learned-skill retention, GPU or full-game claim follows.

The pre-declared experimental patch `fast-write-sham.patch` is source only; it is deliberately not merged into product runtime. `fast-sham-summary.json` contains comparison scalars and hashes of retained local receipts. To prove useful learning, the next controlled test needs a task that requires experience to distinguish alternatives, with real feedback and matched controls, and must measure transfer or reversal rather than merely food completion.
